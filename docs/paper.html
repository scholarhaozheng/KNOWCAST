 a b s t r a c t
In metro operations, Origin-Destination (OD) ﬂows and path ﬂows are critical for transportation
planning, but their accurate short-term forecasting and estimation remain challenging due to the
complex inﬂuencing factors that aﬀect passenger behavior. In addition, due to data collection
challenges, previous research has generally prioritized short-term OD ﬂow forecasting over path
ﬂow estimation. To address these challenges, this paper presents a domain-knowledge-integrated
framework for jointly forecasting short-term network-level OD ﬂows and inferring path ﬂows,
with path-ﬂow inference directly coupled to the predicted OD ﬂows through an embedded assign-
ment mechanism. The framework systematically integrates transportation domain knowledge of
trip generation, trip distribution, and traﬃc  assignment into a sequence-to-sequence architecture.
This integration enables the model to analyze and incorporate these inﬂuencing factors into its
predictions. Furthermore, the proposed adaptive training mechanism progressively reﬁnes the
inﬂuence of these factors by integrating domain-knowledge-based components directly into the
sequence-to-sequence architecture, with key parameters initialized using domain knowledge to
provide an eﬀective starting point for subsequent optimization. The direct coupling between OD
forecasts and inferred path-ﬂow estimates is promoted by leveraging intermediate features that
encode path-level information. Generated by the domain-knowledge-based components during
their joint optimization with the main network, these features are used to produce path-ﬂow
estimates that remain structurally linked to the OD forecasts. The experimental results demon-
strate the eﬀectiveness of the proposed method. By analyzing the optimization trajectories of the
domain-knowledge-derived parameters, this study shows how they evolve during training and
how the deep learning process reﬁnes the initial parameters derived from classical models to
improve predictive performance. The source code used in this study is publicly available at the
GitHub repository: https://github.com/scholarhaozheng/KNOWCAST.

While there is a clear operational need for short-term OD forecasting and path-ﬂow estimation, current research and practice
face four gaps: two practical challenges related to data observability and inﬂuencing-factor integration, and two methodological or
structural limitations in existing modeling approaches.
The ﬁrst gap is a practical observability challenge arising from the limitations of Automated Fare Collection (AFC) records,
the primary data source for metro demand analysis. AFC records capture passengers’ origins, destinations, and transaction times,
but they do not record the speciﬁc routes or trajectories chosen within the network. Emerging sensing technologies can provide
supplementary information, but their use remains constrained in complex underground environments. Wi-Fi and Bluetooth probes,
for example, may not reliably track cross-station trajectories because of MAC address randomization and signal attenuation. Train
load weighing mechanisms and automated door counters provide only indirect approximations of carriage-level ﬂows, since they
rely on static weight assumptions and are vulnerable to cumulative counting errors. As a result, AFC data can identify entry–exit
transactions but cannot directly observe the realized en-route trajectory or path label in a multi-transfer network. Segment-level
ﬂows therefore cannot be directly observed from AFC records. This observability barrier makes joint OD and path-ﬂow estimation an
under-determined problem that is diﬃcult  to resolve using purely data-driven methods alone. Additional structural assumptions are
therefore needed, particularly traﬃc-assignment  principles that regularize the solution space and link OD demand to plausible route
choices.
The second gap concerns the practical diﬃculty  of modeling passenger-ﬂow dynamics, particularly in analyzing and integrating
inﬂuencing factors into predictive models. In addition to the large-scale AFC datasets generated by metro systems, researchers now
have access to external records that describe relevant inﬂuencing factors, such as land use characteristics, catchment population,
operational adjustments, and weather conditions. However, data availability alone does not guarantee high forecasting accuracy.
Simply ingesting these datasets and inﬂuencing factors as ﬂat input features is often insuﬃcient;  an eﬀective model requires a
specialized architecture that can explicitly represent and learn how these factors shape ﬂow dynamics.
The remaining two gaps concern methodological and structural limitations in existing deep learning approaches.
The third gap is that current deep learning models often struggle to capture the eﬀects of dynamic inﬂuencing factors without the
integration of transportation domain knowledge. The core of this issue is methodological: existing approaches lack explicit mecha-
nisms to map these inﬂuencing factors onto behaviorally meaningful determinants (e.g., travel-time perception and transfer penalties).
Rather than using transportation domain knowledge to encode transportation behavioral rules that describe how inﬂuencing factors
(e.g., weather, service disruptions, or operational adjustments) may aﬀect passenger travel behavior, a common approach is to con-
catenate these factors as feature vectors (Liang et al., 2019; Lu et al., 2021). As a result, the network must infer these relationships
implicitly from the training data, which limits interpretability and may reduce its ability to generalize when operational conditions
shift.
The fourth gap lies in the absence of uniﬁed models for simultaneous OD-ﬂow prediction and path-ﬂow estimation. Most state-of-
the-art spatiotemporal frameworks (Gugaliya et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2019c) primarily focus on forecasting the OD matrix, relegating
traﬃc  assignment to a subsequent post-processing step. Such decoupled approaches may not fully capture the dynamic interdependen-
cies between OD demand and network propagation, and thus risk generating path-ﬂow estimates that are operationally inconsistent
with the evolving demand patterns. Overcoming this limitation requires a joint approach that structurally models the interdependen-
cies between OD and path ﬂows based on traﬃc-assignment  principles, thereby improving both model consistency and predictive
performance.
This leads to the central research question of this paper: How can we develop a uniﬁed deep learning framework that leverages
transportation domain knowledge to forecast metro OD ﬂows and simultaneously infer path ﬂows without path-level ground-truth
labels, while maintaining interpretability and adaptability to dynamic inﬂuencing factors?
To bridge these identiﬁed gaps, this paper proposes a domain-knowledge-integrated framework, KNOWCAST (Knowledge-
Integrated OD Forecasting and Path-Flow Estimation), for joint metro network-level OD-ﬂow forecasting and path-ﬂow estimation.
Accordingly, we make the following contributions.
1. To eﬀectively integrate inﬂuencing factors into deep learning models, we apply domain knowledge from transportation planning,
including trip generation, trip distribution, and traﬃc  assignment. For trip generation, we analyze station-level impacts, evaluating
how local socio-demographic proﬁles and major events inﬂuence the passenger volume originating from individual stations. For
trip distribution, we examine how inﬂuencing factors (e.g., line suspensions or service-frequency changes) aﬀect inter-station
travel patterns by altering travel impedance and passenger ﬂows between station pairs. For traﬃc  assignment, we structurally
model the propagation of demand onto network segments, capturing how adjustments to network topology and service frequency
dynamically redirect passenger route choices and alter section-level ﬂows. By translating these inﬂuencing factors into network

2.  Literature review
This section reviews existing methodologies for metro passenger ﬂow forecasting. Table 1 provides a structured overview, cate-
gorizing methods from classical transportation models to modern deep learning approaches and highlighting their treatment of the
challenges that motivate this work: the integration of inﬂuencing factors and the consistent forecasting of OD ﬂows and estimation
of path ﬂows.
As a classic framework in transportation planning, the four-step model oﬀers a theoretically grounded modeling process built
on transportation domain knowledge, beneﬁts from established industry standards, mature calibration techniques, and countless
successful road-network applications. Its principles are also applied to the metro domain, where elements of the model are utilized
to inform planning decisions, even if the entire four-step process is not always implemented in its classical, sequential form (Mo
et al., 2020a; Peng et al., 2024). However, when applied to short-term metro OD ﬂow prediction, the model is not well-equipped to
eﬀectively leverage the real-time OD data generated by the railway system. It is unable to learn directly from the patterns within these
data, nor can it eﬃciently  utilize relevant information concerning dynamic operational changes, such as variations in train service
frequencies or network disruptions (e.g., station or line section closures). Consequently, forecasts derived purely from the four-step
model often exhibit lower accuracy for short-term OD ﬂow prediction compared to methods capable of directly utilizing this detailed
information. Thus, while classical transportation models oﬀer foundational domain knowledge, their practical utility in predicting
metro passenger ﬂow is signiﬁcantly constrained.
To overcome the limitations of classical, domain-knowledge-based methods, statistical (parameterized) models emerged. These
models predict future OD volumes by ﬁtting parameters directly to historical data, without embedding explicit knowledge of network
topology, service schedule, or passenger behavior, and do not rely on externally provided domain knowledge or information from
traﬃc  surveys. The Historical Average (HA) method simply computes the mean volume for each time interval and assumes consistent
temporal patterns, providing a straightforward implementation, but it fails to adapt to sudden demand shifts (Smith and Demetsky,
1997). ARIMA models use auto-regressive and moving-average terms to capture linear temporal dependencies, but are constrained
by assumptions of stationarity, and often face challenges when adapting to abrupt or nonlinear variations (Ahmed and Cook, 1979;
Anvari et al., 2016; Ma et al., 2017; Vlahogianni et al., 2014). Least Squares methods (LS) formulate OD matrix estimation as a
regression problem constrained by link counts and assignment models (Yao et al., 2016). Although these statistical methods can
provide baseline OD forecasts, they can be constrained by their simpliﬁed assumptions. They primarily attempt to extract historical
patterns, but real-world metro passenger ﬂow is frequently inﬂuenced by real-time, non-periodic inﬂuencing factors, which are
common in daily operations. As these models lack mechanisms to account for such disruptions, their practical accuracy is often
compromised.
Machine-learning approaches such as Bayesian networks (BN), Backpropagation Neural Network (BPNN), and Support Vector
Machines (SVMs) have been deployed to forecast metro OD ﬂows by learning complex, non-linear relationships directly from the
data, yet each technique comes with distinctive trade-oﬀs. Bayesian networks encode causal dependencies (e.g., headways, station
connectivity, weather) in a directed acyclic graph and update OD probabilities via Bayes’ rule, allowing heterogeneous evidence fusion
and explicit uncertainty bounds (Blume et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2013; Roos et al., 2017). However, when the number of stations and
features increases, structure learning and exact inference become NP-hard, so analysts must rely on strong independence assumptions
or approximate sampling methods. These shortcuts often reduce accuracy during peak-hour demand surges. Back-propagation neural
networks iteratively adjust weights through gradient back-propagation, capturing the non-linear interplay among timetable changes,
boarding counts, and spatial lags (Roos et al., 2016; Wei and Chen, 2012; Zhou et al., 2016). They can introduce impact features into
training without heavy feature engineering. However, a plain feed-forward network has no explicit temporal memory, so it struggles to

However, despite the impressive predictive capabilities of deep learning models, a closer examination reveals a limitation in
how these models incorporate inﬂuencing factors. Real-world metro operations are constantly inﬂuenced by a variety of inﬂuencing
factors, such as weather conditions (Guo et al., 2020), air quality (Zhang et al., 2020), day of the week (Zhang et al., 2017), holidays
(Chen et al., 2019), and events (Chen et al., 2019), which require adaptive management strategies. These passenger ﬂow management
strategies can directly or indirectly inﬂuence the metro service network, as well as change how passengers enter and move through
the system. The eﬀectiveness of a predictive model therefore depends on whether it can remain reliable during the disruptions that
commonly occur in daily operations. Performance under incident-free settings alone is not suﬃcient.  To account for inﬂuencing factors
aﬀecting OD ﬂow distribution, previous studies have explored diﬀerent methods to integrate these factors into deep learning-based
passenger OD ﬂow forecasting models. The predominant method for incorporating these factors is to embed them as unstructured
augmented feature vectors and concatenate them with other input, either with the primary input tensor (Liang et al., 2019; Liu
et al., 2019a; Lu et al., 2021; Yao et al., 2019), or with the encoded spatiotemporal features before the ﬁnal prediction layer (Zhang
et al., 2020, 2017, 2018, 2019b). This approach, while straightforward in providing the model with raw information, generally
does not explicitly incorporate transportation domain knowledge to deﬁne how these inﬂuencing factors logically shape passenger
travel behavior, such as trip generation or distribution patterns. Consequently, the model must infer these complex relationships
from correlations in the training data and the directly incorporated, unanalyzed inﬂuencing factors alone. This inference process is
often challenging. A few studies have begun to address this gap by using domain knowledge to guide the analysis and integration of
inﬂuencing factors. For example, Zhang et al. (2023b) employs a passenger travel path choice model to calculate the passenger ﬂow
volumes on a speciﬁc route section. Then it calculates the relative importance of OD pairs and incorporates this importance into the
loss function to guide the optimization direction. However, these knowledge-guided approaches remain the exception.
Beyond the challenge of knowledge integration, the fundamental limitation is that current models are primarily designed to predict
overall passenger demand (OD ﬂows), and in doing so, largely neglect the prediction of how that traﬃc  will spread out across speciﬁc
routes (path ﬂows). As summarized in Table 1, most advanced deep learning models, including the spatiotemporal frameworks cited
earlier, deﬁne their primary objective as predicting the OD matrix. They largely overlook the task of producing path-ﬂow estimates
that remain directly coupled to the OD results. If these two tasks are separated, a potential limitation of this separation is that it may
not fully capture the strong inter-dependencies between OD and path ﬂows. The challenge of ensuring their consistency is explicitly
recognized in transportation science. For instance, some researchers have focused on the joint estimation of OD matrices and path
ﬂows to ﬁnd a mutually consistent solution, often using classical optimization (Tsekeris et al., 2007). However, these methods are
often distinct from the end-to-end deep learning paradigm. Within the deep learning context, where models are trained directly on
data, forecasting OD ﬂows and estimating path ﬂows with separate, uncoordinated models creates the risk of generating logically
inconsistent and operationally unreliable outputs. Therefore, a critical research gap exists: the absence of a uniﬁed deep learning
framework capable of jointly forecasting OD ﬂows and inferring path ﬂows while keeping the two outputs directly coupled within a
single model.
This review thus conﬁrms the two primary methodological gaps identiﬁed in Section 1. First, we have seen that while deep learn-
ing models are powerful, they primarily treat inﬂuencing factors as unstructured inputs, failing to leverage transportation domain
knowledge for more robust and interpretable predictions. Second, the literature shows a clear and persistent focus on OD ﬂow fore-
casting, with a signiﬁcant lack of uniﬁed frameworks that can ensure consistency between OD predictions and path ﬂow estimations.
Our work is therefore positioned to directly address these limitations by proposing a knowledge-integrated architecture for joint
forecasting and estimation.
3.  Framework for integrating domain knowledge into forecasting models
The KNOWCAST framework integrates domain knowledge from classical transportation models (including trip generation, trip
distribution, and traﬃc  assignment) into its deep learning architecture through its core component, the Knowledge-Integrated Traﬃc
Predictor (KITP). These KITPs serve as the building blocks for both the encoder and decoder in KNOWCAST’s sequence-to-sequence
(Seq2Seq) architecture. Embedding KITP units throughout the encoder–decoder structure allows KNOWCAST to leverage intermediate
representations and outputs generated during OD forecasting, such as path-choice probabilities, to infer path ﬂows even when direct
path-ﬂow observations are unavailable.

Expanding upon the initial roadmap, Fig. 2 presents the detailed methodology for knowledge integration. In deep learning, a model
𝑀 comprises several components: a dataset 𝐷, a network architecture 𝜋, a loss function L , and a set of parameters 𝜃. The training
process is to learn the optimal parameters 𝜃 for a model 𝑀, which is achieved by using a learner 𝐿 to minimize a loss function L on the
given dataset 𝐷 (Dash et al., 2022). Within this context, we deﬁne knowledge integration as the process of translating problem-speciﬁc
domain knowledge into targeted modiﬁcations of one or more of these components: the data 𝐷, the architecture 𝜋, the parameters
𝜃, or the loss function L , and integrating them into the model. This translation and integration are performed by what we term a
Knowledge Integrator (as indicated by À, Á, Â, Ã, and Ä).
Our four-layered methodology for knowledge integration is detailed as follows:
(a) Raw Data: Presents the initial model inputs, including historical OD data and various inﬂuencing factors.
(b) Domain Knowledge: Represents the repository of domain knowledge, including transportation principles and parameters.
(c) Knowledge Integrator: Employing domain knowledge to analyze the impacts of inﬂuencing factors on the metro system and
translate these impacts into components that can be directly integrated into deep learning models. We formalize this into four
primary methods of knowledge integration:
(i) Method 1: Data Transformation: Wherein domain knowledge is analyzed to process relevant inﬂuencing factors and extend
the original data 𝐷 with domain-knowledge-integrated features, produce domain-knowledge-enriched data 𝐷′, which serves
as the actual input to the model.
(ii) Method 2: Loss Function Modiﬁcation: Wherein knowledge-informed terms are integrated into the original loss function L ,
forming an enhanced loss function L ′ that incorporates domain-theoretical guidance during model optimization.
(iii) Method 3: Architecture Extension: Wherein domain knowledge extends the model’s architecture 𝜋 into 𝜋′ by incorporating
a knowledge-based component.
(iv) Method 4: Parameter Initialization: Wherein domain knowledge provides initial values for a subset of the knowledge-
based component’s parameters 𝜃, allowing the model to begin with a transportation theory-based foundation and optimize
these parameters dynamically during training. This process creates domain-knowledge-informed parameters 𝜃′, which are

timate of OD proportions. The derived OD proportions are appended to the OD feature vector to improve sensitivity to network-wide

4.  Key techniques of knowledge-integrated traﬃc  predictor (KITP)
The Knowledge-Integrated Traﬃc  Predictor (KITP) is the central component of the KNOWCAST framework. It incorporates domain
knowledge into a network-level predictive model using the previously mentioned Knowledge Integrators. They are designed to analyze
and integrate inﬂuencing factors by performing separate tasks such as enriching input features by capturing temporal regularities and
modeling inter-station spatial correlations with a gravity model, introducing knowledge-informed loss terms, and utilizing dynamic
network structures for knowledge-informed parameter initializations. The resulting knowledge-integrated features, loss functions, and
initialized parameters are then leveraged by a Graph Attention Gated Recurrent Unit (GATRU) to jointly produce knowledge-aware
network-level OD forecasting and path ﬂow estimation.
4.1.  Knowledge-driven modular design for enhanced ﬂow forecasting and estimation
As illustrated in Fig. 4, the KITP’s architecture is an implementation of the methodological framework introduced in Section 3.1
(see Fig. 2). Its horizontal layers directly correspond to the conceptual stages of (a) Raw Data; (b) Domain Knowledge (where Layer
b.1 represents traﬃc-domain  parameters, e.g., the initial coeﬃcients  for a path utility function; Layer b.2 describes the transportation
behavioral rules, e.g., the functional form of a gravity model for trip distribution); (c) Knowledge Integrator; and (d) Learner.
Layer c is the action layer where domain knowledge from Layer b is applied to the data from Layer a. Through a series of
transformations, the domain knowledge is applied to assess the impact on the metro system by performing linear transformations,
arithmetic operations, and neural network transformations. Layer c in Fig. 4 is vertically organized into ﬁve Knowledge Integrators
to perform separate tasks:
(1) Allocating Unﬁnished OD Flow and Aggregating the Resulting OD Matrix: Component À captures temporal regularities from
historical OD ﬂow data through periodic windowing and by addressing data sparsity via matrix aggregation.
(2) Formulating a Trip Generation-Informed Loss Term: Component Á formulates a trip generation-informed loss term based on
independently modeled station-level trip generation estimation, to guide the optimization process.
(3) Generating OD-Distribution-Based Augmented Features: Component Â generates an OD-Distribution-Based Adjustment Matrix
to serve as an augmented feature for the primary OD matrix, thereby informing the model about how inter-station travel patterns
are likely to shift due to these inﬂuences.
(4) Incorporating Path-Choice Components into Neural Networks with Knowledge-Based Initialization: Component Ã functions as
a diﬀerentiable assignment layer. It leverages domain knowledge of traﬃc  assignment to both incorporate a logit-model-based
component into the deep learning framework and initialize a set of trainable parameters with knowledge-informed values. This
design allows the model to infer path ﬂows directly from OD forecasts without requiring path-level ground truth, ensuring that
the estimated ﬂows remain behaviorally consistent with the predicted OD demand.
(5) Incorporating Service-Network Variations and Their Impacts on Passenger Path Choice Modeling: Component Ä augments the
OD features using compressed feature representations of path-level choices and service frequency. These representations are
generated from network-level service information.
Taken together, the predictor-side OD feature representation consists of the primary historical OD-ﬂow input and three knowledge-
integrated augmentations: the OD-distribution-based feature from Component Â, the compressed path-choice representation from
Component Ã/Ä, and the compressed service-frequency representation from Component Ä. Thus, the historical OD input provides
the demand baseline, while the three augmented components respectively encode distributional shifts, path-choice information, and
time-varying service-network conditions.
Beyond feature extraction, these Knowledge Integrators serve a critical function by acting as structural regularizers. While tradi-
tional deep learning often requires explicit regularization such as 𝐿1 or 𝐿2 norms to prevent overﬁtting, the integration of theoretical
models in KNOWCAST imposes strong inductive biases that constrain the neural network’s hypothesis space. For instance, the Logit-
based path choice component (Component Ã) and the Gravity-based distribution logic (Component Â) force the model to seek
solutions adhering to transportation principles rather than memorizing high-frequency noise or idiosyncratic patterns in the training

4.3.  Formulating a trip generation-informed loss term (component Á)
To improve training eﬃciency  by encouraging the model to generate OD ﬂows that align with aggregate station-level trip produc-
tion and attraction estimates, we introduce a domain-knowledge-integrated penalty term into the loss function. This penalty measures
the divergence between the predicted OD ﬂows and independently estimated station-level trip generation volumes. By incorporating
this loss term, the model is encouraged to align its predictions with domain-knowledge-informed expectations, particularly during
the early stages of training.
To further improve training eﬃciency,  we adopt a Progressive Loss Weighting strategy. This approach gradually adjusts the relative
inﬂuence of diﬀerent loss components during training. It allows the model to ﬁrst focus on the more straightforward task of matching
trip generation volumes before progressively shifting attention to the more complex objective of accurate OD ﬂow prediction. This
learning process facilitates better convergence and performance.

4.3.2.  Employing DCRNN to capture the spatiotemporal dependencies in trip generation
To ensure that localized disruptions in passenger ﬂow are eﬀectively captured in OD ﬂow predictions, we incorporate station-
level dynamics into the OD forecasting process. We independently forecast the trip generation and attraction at each station and
use the divergence between these estimates and the predicted OD ﬂows as domain-knowledge-integrated loss terms. These terms are
integrated into the loss function to guide the training process, thereby improving the model’s sensitivity to deviations from regular
cyclical patterns.
We begin by representing the spatial structure for trip generation as a weighted directed graph 𝐺TAZ = (𝑉TAZ, 𝐸TAZ, 𝑊TAZ). 𝑉TAZ
denotes a set of vertices, where each vertex 𝑣 ∈ 𝑉TAZ corresponds to a Traﬃc  Analysis Zone (TAZ) centered around a metro station.

denotes the physical metro links connecting these zones, and 𝑊TAZ represents the weighted adjacency matrix governing the spatial
diﬀusion process in the DCRNN. The model is driven by features of each station’s TAZ, which are used to model trip attraction and
trip production.

4.3.3.  Applying progressive loss weighting
To leverage the trip generation domain knowledge to guide the model’s initial learning without compromising the ﬁnal perfor-
mance on the OD prediction task, we adopt a Progressive Loss Weighting strategy (Chen et al., 2018; Guo et al., 2018; Heydari et al.,
2019; Liu et al., 2019b). This approach addresses the challenges of training instability that often arise when directly optimizing the
highly complex OD prediction task. It enables the model’s attention to gradually transition from the simpler trip generation task to
the more complex OD prediction task.
Speciﬁcally, during the early stages of training, we assign a higher weight to the trip generation-informed loss term. As training
progresses, the weight of the trip generation-informed loss term is linearly decayed, shifting the optimization focus toward the core
OD prediction task. This strategy allows the model to fully leverage the domain knowledge from the trip generation phase while
avoiding being overwhelmed by the optimization complexity of OD prediction in the early stages, thereby promoting more stable
and eﬃcient  convergence.

4.5.  Incorporating path-choice components into neural networks with knowledge-based initialization (component Ã)
To account for the inﬂuence of dynamic metro service-network structures (e.g., station or section closures and the operation of
short-turning services) on passenger path choice, this component integrates a Logit-based component into the main neural network
and utilizes domain-knowledge-informed parameter initialization. The Logit-based component explicitly represents the passenger
path-choice process as a diﬀerentiable operation within the computation graph. It introduces an input layer, a utility layer, and a
Multinomial Logit (Softmax) layer to compute path-choice probabilities for each OD pair. By embedding this logic, the framework
transforms path-ﬂow estimation into a transportation-domain-knowledge-guided inference task, helping address the lack of path-level
ground truth.
After determining path choice probabilities for OD pairs, we calculate the probability that a path for any given OD pair will
traverse each speciﬁc section of the network. This section-level probability establishes a direct link to variations in the service
network. Changes in the service network directly aﬀect the operating condition of these sections (e.g., section availability and service
frequency), which in turn inﬂuences path utility and feasible path selection. Therefore, the section traversal probabilities calculated
by this module will be utilized by other Knowledge Integrators to model the impact of service network variations on passenger path
choice within our predictive framework.

4.8.  Deep spatio-temporal learner using graph attention gated recurrent units (GATRU)
To capture spatial-temporal dependencies in metro ridership and fully leverage the components that Knowledge Integrators gen-
erate, we design a learner based on the Graph Attention-based Gated Recurrent (GATRU) Units. The GATRU unit combines a Graph
Attention Network (GAT) and a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) (Dey and Salem, 2017). The GAT aggregates neighbor information into
station-level features, while the GRU captures the temporal evolution of ridership patterns and generates hidden states for all nodes
over time.
The metro system is modeled as a directed graph 𝐺 = (𝑉 , ), following prior work (Chen et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2023a;
Zong et al., 2025). 𝑉  denotes the set of vertices representing metro stations, with |STN| stations in total, and  denotes the set
of directed physical connections between stations. To support graph-attention computation, we deﬁne a binary adjacency matrix

resentation of the metro topology and to deﬁne the feasible attention neighborhood for each station. In the GAT layer, the adjacency
structure does not prescribe ﬁxed propagation weights. Instead, it masks the feasible station-to-station connections, while the relative
contribution of each neighboring station is learned through attention coeﬃcients  normalized over the corresponding neighborhood.

5.1.4.  Parameter settings and tuning
In urban metro transit path-choice models, two key negative-utility parameters are the per-station disutility (𝛽stations ), which
expresses the negative utility experienced by travelers for each station traversed on their journey, and the transfer penalty (𝛽transfer ),
which represents the disutility associated with the eﬀort of making a transfer between lines. Empirical studies estimate 𝛽transfer ≈
−0.573, based on revealed-preference surveys (Mo et al., 2021, 2023). For per-station disutility, however, direct estimates are less
commonly reported; this eﬀect is often reﬂected indirectly through in-vehicle time (IVT) coeﬃcients.  By combining the IVT coeﬃcient
𝛽ivt = −0.147 (per minute) (Mo et al., 2023), with the average station-to-station travel time (approximately 2 min), a reasonable
per-station disutility is computed by 𝛽stations ≈ −0.147 × 2 ≈ −0 .294, rounded to −0.30. This reﬂects that each station stop imposes
measurable waiting, boarding/alighting delays, and crowding costs (Mo et al., 2023; Su et al., 2023). Additional studies (Chan et al.,
2021; Mo et al., 2020b; Su et al., 2023) corroborate this -0.30 magnitude. Consequently, domain-parameter values are initialized to
𝛽transfer = −0.573 (per transfer), 𝛽stations = −0.30 (per station).
Bayesian optimization (Frazier, 2018) was employed to ﬁne-tune several critical hyperparameters, targeting minimization of the
validation MAE. We used the automatic hyperparameter optimization framework, Optuna, particularly designed for machine learning.
All Optuna studies were conducted using the SQLite storage backend and the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) sampler with
a MedianPruner for early stopping (Akiba et al., 2019; Bischl et al., 2023). During each trial, model training was restricted to 40
epochs. A total of 80 trials were executed. After completion of the optimization, the best hyperparameter conﬁguration was re-
inserted into the original conﬁguration ﬁle, and a ﬁnal full-length training run was performed. The best validation MAE across the
80 Bayesian-optimization trials was achieved at Trial 78.
The hyperparameter search space and the corresponding ﬁnal optimized values are reported in Table 2. In the main comparative
experiments, the OD matrix dimension retention ratio was ﬁxed at 0.23 for all models to ensure that all benchmark, ablation, and
full-model comparisons were conducted on the same prediction target and under the same information-retention level. In addition,

6.  Conclusion
In this study, we proposed KNOWCAST, a domain-knowledge-integrated framework for joint short-term network-level OD-ﬂow
forecasting and path-ﬂow estimation in urban metro systems. The framework combines transportation domain knowledge with the
data-driven strengths of deep learning by embedding knowledge from trip generation, trip distribution, and traﬃc  assignment into
a sequence-to-sequence architecture with GATRU as the core learning component. The integration of domain knowledge at both the
architectural and parameter-initialization levels improves the framework’s practical adaptability and interpretability. The knowledge-
informed architecture embeds transportation mechanisms into the prediction process, while knowledge-derived initialization provides
selected trainable parameters with behaviorally meaningful starting values that can be reﬁned during training. The same design also
supports joint OD-ﬂow forecasting and path-ﬂow estimation. Intermediate representations generated by the knowledge-integrated
components are passed to the embedded diﬀerentiable assignment layer, allowing path-ﬂow estimates to be inferred from predicted
OD demand without path-level ground-truth labels while remaining structurally coupled to the OD forecasts. These inferred path-ﬂow
estimates can then be aggregated to obtain section-level ﬂow estimates, providing operationally useful information for metro manage-
ment. However, direct validation of path-ﬂow estimates remains challenging because of the observability gap in urban metro systems.
AFC records reveal entry–exit transactions but do not identify the realized routes, while supplementary sensing technologies such
as Wi-Fi or Bluetooth probes often cannot reliably reconstruct complete in-network trajectories. Therefore, the path-ﬂow estimates
in this study should be interpreted as structurally constrained estimates rather than directly observed quantities. Their plausibil-
ity is supported by the embedded Logit-based assignment structure, which allocates predicted OD demand across topology-feasible
candidate paths according to path-choice probabilities.
Our numerical experiments on a real-world dataset from the City S Metro demonstrate the eﬀectiveness of KNOWCAST relative
to ten state-of-the-art baseline models across multiple evaluation metrics, including Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square
Error (RMSE), and Weighted Mean Absolute Percentage Error (WMAPE). To systematically quantify the accuracy improvements from
integrating diﬀerent Knowledge Integrators, we conducted a series of ablation studies. These studies showed that the Knowledge In-
tegrators contribute in diﬀerent ways: some directly improve ﬁnal predictive accuracy, while others primarily enhance convergence
stability, reduce large-error outliers, or provide stronger structural guidance during training. The segmented error analysis further
conﬁrms the robustness of KNOWCAST under the long-tail demand distribution. The model captures high-density trunk ﬂows while
maintaining low non-zero predictions in sparse OD groups. The zero-ﬂow analysis further shows that domain-knowledge integration
supports global network reliability without overﬁtting to low-volume noise. The architecture of KNOWCAST also provides potential
for scalability and generalizability. Regarding scalability, KNOWCAST leverages Graph Attention-based Gated Recurrent Units that
focus on local neighborhood dependencies, reducing reliance on fully connected station-to-station interactions as network size in-
creases. Regarding generalizability, KNOWCAST is topology-aware because it embeds the logic of trip generation, trip distribution,
and traﬃc  assignment. By utilizing socio-demographic covariates, network topology, and assignment constraints as structural inputs,
KNOWCAST remains sensitive to changes such as new line openings or revised TAZ-like station catchment deﬁnitions. Although the
current study validates the model in a single city, this mechanism-informed and modular design suggests potential adaptability to
network-evolution scenarios and other urban network topologies. The use of trainable parameters initialized from domain knowledge
further strengthens interpretability. These parameters provide behaviorally meaningful starting values for optimization, while their
post-training trajectories oﬀer an interpretable record of how the data support, reﬁne, or depart from the original transportation-
theory priors. Accordingly, the ﬁne-tuned parameter values should be interpreted in the context of the forecasting task and the studied
network, as calibrated operational coeﬃcients  rather than direct estimates of universally stable behavioral preferences. More broadly,
the knowledge-informed components act as structural regularizers by constraining the optimization toward transportation-consistent
solutions and reducing the tendency to overﬁt low-volume patterns in the training data.
Overall, the results show that integrating transportation domain knowledge into deep learning can improve OD-ﬂow forecasting
while enabling structurally consistent path-ﬂow estimation. This integration also supports the development of more interpretable,
robust, and data-eﬃcient  models for metro network analysis. Although this study validates the overall eﬀectiveness of KNOWCAST,
its complex multi-component architecture leaves several issues for further investigation. Future work should conduct more compre-
hensive sensitivity analyses to quantify the model’s dependence on domain-knowledge-based parameter initialization and to examine
nonlinear interactions among diﬀerent Knowledge Integrators. The framework could also be extended to more complex operational
scenarios, such as forecasting metro passenger-ﬂow changes during emergency evacuations, to assess and improve model resilience
under disruptive conditions.
